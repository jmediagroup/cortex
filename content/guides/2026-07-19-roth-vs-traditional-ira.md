Both accounts let you invest for retirement with tax advantages the IRS doesn't offer on a regular brokerage account. The difference comes down to when you pay taxes: now, or later. That single choice affects how much you can contribute, whether you get a deduction today, and how your withdrawals are taxed decades from now.

## The core tax difference

A traditional IRA is funded with pre-tax (or tax-deductible) dollars. You may get a deduction the year you contribute, your money grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax dollars. You get no deduction now, but qualified withdrawals in retirement — including all the growth — are completely tax-free.

In short: traditional IRA means "pay taxes later," Roth IRA means "pay taxes now." Which is better depends largely on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher or lower in retirement than it is today. If you expect to be in a lower bracket later, the traditional IRA's upfront deduction is more valuable. If you expect a similar or higher bracket later — common for younger savers early in their careers — the Roth's tax-free withdrawals tend to win out.

## 2026 contribution limits

For 2026, the annual contribution limit for IRAs rises to $7,500 (up from $7,000 in 2025), with a $1,100 catch-up contribution available to savers age 50 and older, for a total of $8,600.

This limit is combined across account types — if you contribute to both a Roth and a traditional IRA in the same year, your total contributions across both accounts still can't exceed $7,500 (or $8,600 with the catch-up).

## Who can contribute — and who can deduct

**Roth IRA income limits.** Unlike a traditional IRA, eligibility to contribute directly to a Roth IRA phases out at higher incomes. For 2026, the phase-out range is $153,000–$168,000 for single filers and heads of household, and $242,000–$252,000 for married couples filing jointly. Below the range you can contribute the full amount; inside the range your allowed contribution shrinks; above it, you can't contribute directly (though a "backdoor Roth" conversion is a common workaround for high earners — talk to a tax professional before attempting one, since the mechanics involve other IRA balances and pro-rata rules).

**Traditional IRA deductibility.** Anyone with earned income can contribute to a traditional IRA regardless of income, but the deduction is a separate question. If neither you nor your spouse has access to a workplace retirement plan (like a 401(k)), your full contribution is deductible no matter your income. If you're covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out between $81,000–$91,000 for single filers and $129,000–$149,000 for married couples filing jointly in 2026. If you're not covered but your spouse is, the phase-out range is $242,000–$252,000.

## Withdrawal rules and required distributions

This is where the accounts diverge most in practice, especially later in life.

- **Required minimum distributions (RMDs):** Traditional IRAs require you to start withdrawing a minimum amount each year once you reach age 73. Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the original owner's lifetime, which makes them a useful tool for legacy planning or simply keeping money invested longer.
- **Early withdrawal penalty:** Withdrawals before age 59½ generally trigger a 10% penalty on top of any taxes owed, with exceptions for situations like a first-time home purchase or disability. Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time, tax- and penalty-free, since you already paid tax on that money.
- **The 5-year rule:** For a Roth IRA, earnings can only be withdrawn tax-free if the account has been open at least 5 years AND you're 59½ or older (or meet another exception). Each Roth conversion also starts its own 5-year clock for that converted amount.

## How to decide

There's no universally correct answer, but a few rules of thumb help:

- **Early career, lower income:** A Roth often makes sense — you're likely in a lower tax bracket now than you will be later, so paying tax today is relatively cheap.
- **Peak earning years, higher tax bracket:** A traditional IRA's deduction is worth more right now, especially if you expect your retirement income (and tax bracket) to be lower.
- **Want flexibility and no forced withdrawals:** The Roth's lack of RMDs and penalty-free access to contributions make it more flexible.
- **Not sure which bracket you'll land in:** Splitting contributions between both account types is a reasonable hedge — it diversifies your future tax exposure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I have both a Roth and a traditional IRA?

Yes. You can contribute to both in the same year, but your combined contributions across all your IRAs still can't exceed the annual limit ($7,500 for 2026, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older).

### Is a Roth IRA better than a 401(k) match?

No — if your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute enough to get the full match first. That's an immediate, guaranteed return that no IRA can match. After capturing the match, deciding between additional 401(k) contributions and an IRA becomes a separate question of account type, investment options, and fees.

### What happens if I contribute to a Roth IRA above the income limit?

Excess contributions are subject to a 6% excise tax for each year they remain in the account unless corrected. You generally need to withdraw the excess (and any earnings on it) before your tax filing deadline to avoid the penalty, or recharacterize the contribution as a traditional IRA contribution.

### Can I convert a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA?

Yes, through a Roth conversion. You'll owe ordinary income tax on the converted amount in the year of conversion, but the money then grows tax-free going forward, subject to its own 5-year holding period. This is a common strategy for managing future tax brackets, but the tax bill from a large conversion can be significant, so it's worth planning carefully.

### Do Roth and traditional IRAs invest in the same things?

Yes — the tax treatment is the only real difference. Both account types can generally hold the same range of investments, including stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and ETFs, through most brokerages.
