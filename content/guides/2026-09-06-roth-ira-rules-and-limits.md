A Roth IRA is one of the few accounts in the tax code that lets your money grow and come out completely tax-free in retirement. In exchange, you give up a deduction today. Whether that trade makes sense depends on your income, your tax bracket now versus later, and whether you can contribute directly at all. This guide covers the rules you need to actually use one well.

## How a Roth IRA works

You fund a Roth IRA with money you've already paid income tax on. There's no upfront deduction. In exchange, the account grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement — both your original contributions and all the investment growth on top of them — come out with no tax owed at all.

That's the opposite of a Traditional IRA, which gives you a deduction on the way in but taxes withdrawals as ordinary income in retirement. Both share the same combined annual contribution limit, so if you have both account types, your total contributions across them can't exceed the yearly cap.

For 2026, the contribution limit is $7,500 for those under 50, and $8,600 for those 50 and older (a $1,100 catch-up contribution). Contributions for a given tax year can be made up until the tax-filing deadline the following spring — for the 2026 tax year, that's April 15, 2027. You need earned income (wages or self-employment income) at least equal to whatever you contribute.

Because the growth compounds tax-free for decades, small differences in how early you start add up. ## Who can contribute directly

Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes, based on your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). For 2026:

- **Single filers:** full contribution allowed below $153,000; a partial (reduced) contribution is allowed between $153,000 and $168,000; no direct contribution is allowed at or above $168,000.
- **Married filing jointly:** full contribution allowed below $242,000; a partial contribution between $242,000 and $252,000; no direct contribution at or above $252,000.

If your income falls in the phase-out range, the maximum amount you can contribute shrinks gradually rather than dropping to zero all at once. If your income is above the upper end of the range, you're locked out of contributing directly — but not necessarily locked out of a Roth IRA entirely (see the backdoor strategy below).

## Withdrawal rules and the five-year clock

Roth IRA withdrawal rules split contributions and earnings into two different buckets, and the IRS always treats withdrawals as coming from contributions first.

**Contributions** — the money you actually put in — can be withdrawn at any time, for any reason, with no tax and no penalty. You already paid tax on that money, so the IRS doesn't touch it again.

**Earnings** — the investment growth on top of your contributions — are a different story. To withdraw earnings tax- and penalty-free, you need a "qualified" distribution, which requires both of the following:

1. **The five-year rule:** at least five years must have passed since January 1 of the tax year of your first Roth contribution. This clock starts once, the first time you fund any Roth IRA, and it runs regardless of your age.
2. **A qualifying event:** you're 59½ or older, permanently disabled, using up to a $10,000 lifetime amount toward a first home purchase, or the withdrawal is made by a beneficiary after your death.

If you withdraw earnings before satisfying both conditions, that portion is taxed as income and typically hit with a 10% early withdrawal penalty. The IRS does carve out penalty (though not always tax) exceptions for situations like qualified higher-education expenses, birth or adoption costs, certain medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI, and a few others.

One nuance worth knowing: converted balances (see backdoor Roth, below) each have their own five-year clock for penalty purposes, separate from the account's overall five-year rule.

## The backdoor Roth IRA for high earners

If your income is above the direct-contribution limits, you can still get money into a Roth IRA through a "backdoor" conversion. The mechanics are simple: you contribute after-tax (non-deductible) money to a Traditional IRA, then convert that Traditional IRA balance to a Roth IRA. Since there's no income limit on Roth *conversions* — only on direct contributions — this is fully legal and widely used.

The complication is the **pro-rata rule**. If you have other pre-tax money sitting in any Traditional IRA (including old rollover 401(k)s), the IRS doesn't let you convert only the after-tax sliver you just contributed. Instead, every conversion is taxed in proportion to the pre-tax and after-tax share of your *combined* Traditional IRA balances. For example, if you have $93,000 in pre-tax IRA money and add a $7,000 non-deductible contribution, only 7% of anything you convert comes out tax-free — the rest is taxed as ordinary income.

The common workaround is to roll existing pre-tax IRA balances into an employer 401(k) plan (if it accepts incoming rollovers) before doing the backdoor conversion, which removes that pre-tax money from the pro-rata calculation entirely.

## Roth IRA versus other retirement accounts

A Roth IRA is not a replacement for a workplace retirement plan — it's a complement to one. Many savers use both: contribute enough to a 401(k) to capture any employer match, then direct additional savings to a Roth IRA for tax diversification in retirement. Because a Roth IRA has no employer match and a lower contribution limit than a 401(k), it typically makes sense as a second stop rather than the first.

## Common mistakes

- **Contributing above the income limit without realizing it.** MAGI is calculated after certain adjustments, and a year-end bonus or freelance income can push you over the line unexpectedly. If that happens, you can recharacterize or withdraw the excess contribution before the tax deadline to avoid a 6% excise tax penalty on the excess amount.
- **Assuming the five-year clock resets with each contribution.** It doesn't — the clock starts once, with your very first Roth contribution, and covers the account for as long as you hold it.
- **Ignoring the pro-rata rule before doing a backdoor Roth.** If you have any pre-tax IRA balances anywhere, converting will trigger a partially taxable event no matter how small your new non-deductible contribution is.
- **Withdrawing earnings early without checking for an exception.** Contributions come out tax- and penalty-free, but earnings withdrawn before you meet the five-year rule and a qualifying event are taxed and often penalized — know which bucket you're pulling from.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I contribute to a Roth IRA and a 401(k) in the same year?

Yes. They're separate accounts with separate limits, and contributing to a 401(k) doesn't affect your Roth IRA eligibility (aside from the general income phase-out that applies regardless of your 401(k) participation).

### What happens if I contribute too much to my Roth IRA?

Excess contributions are subject to a 6% excise tax for every year they remain in the account. You can avoid the penalty by withdrawing the excess amount (plus any earnings on it) before your tax-filing deadline, or by recharacterizing the contribution.

### Is the backdoor Roth IRA legal?

Yes. It's a widely used, IRS-acknowledged strategy that combines a non-deductible Traditional IRA contribution with a Roth conversion. The pro-rata rule determines how much of the conversion is taxable, but the strategy itself isn't a gray area.

### Can I withdraw my Roth IRA contributions before retirement?

Yes, your original contributions can be withdrawn at any time, for any reason, without tax or penalty, because you already paid tax on that money before contributing it. Only the earnings portion is subject to the five-year rule and qualifying-event requirements.

### Do Roth IRAs have required minimum distributions (RMDs)?

No. Unlike Traditional IRAs and most 401(k)s, Roth IRAs do not require you to take minimum distributions during your lifetime, which makes them useful for leaving tax-free money to heirs or simply letting the balance keep growing.
