A 401(k) is the most common way Americans save for retirement, and it's also one of the most misunderstood pieces of a paycheck. The rules around contribution limits, employer matching, vesting, and the Roth-vs-traditional choice all affect how much money actually ends up in your account decades from now. This guide walks through how the plan works mechanically, what the 2026 numbers are, and the decisions worth getting right early.

## What a 401(k) actually is

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement account. Money comes out of your paycheck before you ever see it, goes into investments you choose from a menu your employer's plan offers, and grows tax-advantaged until retirement. The tax advantage is the whole point: contributions to a traditional 401(k) reduce your taxable income today, and the money isn't taxed again until you withdraw it in retirement.

Many employers also add money on top of what you contribute — an employer match — which is effectively part of your compensation that only shows up if you contribute enough to claim it.

## 2026 contribution limits

The IRS adjusts 401(k) limits most years for inflation. For 2026:

| Limit | 2026 amount |
|---|---|
| Employee salary deferral limit | $24,500 |
| Catch-up contribution (age 50+) | additional $8,000 |
| "Super" catch-up (ages 60–63) | additional $11,250, in place of the standard catch-up |
| Combined employee + employer limit | $72,000 (or 100% of compensation, whichever is lower) |
| Traditional/Roth IRA limit (separate account) | $7,500 |
| IRA catch-up (age 50+) | additional $1,100 |

The employee deferral limit is what you personally can put in through payroll deductions. The combined limit is higher because it also counts employer matching and other employer contributions. If you're 50 or older, you can contribute more through catch-up contributions — and if you're between 60 and 63, current rules let you contribute even more through a higher "super" catch-up amount, assuming your plan offers it.

These figures typically move a little each year with inflation, so it's worth checking the current-year limit rather than assuming last year's number still applies.

## How employer matching and vesting work

An employer match is money your company contributes based on how much you put in yourself. Common formulas include a full dollar-for-dollar match up to some percentage of salary (say, the first 3%), sometimes followed by a partial match on the next few percent, or a flat 50% match up to a set percentage of pay. The exact formula is spelled out in your plan documents.

Two things trip people up:

- **Not contributing enough to get the full match.** If your plan matches 50 cents on the dollar up to 6% of pay and you're only contributing 3%, you're leaving free money on the table — an instant, guaranteed return that doesn't require picking the right investment.
- **Vesting schedules.** The money you contribute yourself is always 100% yours. Employer contributions may vest on a schedule instead — either "cliff" vesting (you own 0% until a certain point, like three years, then 100% all at once) or "graded" vesting (you own a rising percentage each year). Leaving a job before you're fully vested means forfeiting some or all of the unvested employer money, which is worth checking before changing jobs.

## Traditional vs. Roth 401(k)

Most plans now offer both a traditional and a Roth option, and the choice comes down to when you'd rather pay taxes.

**Traditional 401(k):** contributions are pre-tax, which lowers your taxable income now. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. This tends to favor people who expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement than they are today, or who want the immediate tax break.

**Roth 401(k):** contributions are made with after-tax dollars, so there's no upfront deduction. Qualified withdrawals in retirement — generally once you're 59½ and have held the account at least five years — are completely tax-free, including all the growth. This tends to favor people earlier in their careers who expect to be in a higher bracket later, or who simply want tax-free income in retirement.

Some people split contributions between both to hedge against not knowing what tax rates will look like decades from now. ## Choosing investments inside your plan

A 401(k) is an account type, not an investment itself — you still have to choose what to hold inside it, usually from a limited menu your employer selects. Common options include:

- **Target-date funds**, which automatically shift from growth-oriented to conservative holdings as you approach a target retirement year. These are a reasonable single-fund default for people who don't want to manage allocation themselves.
- **Index funds**, tracking a broad market benchmark like the S&P 500 or total U.S. stock market, generally at low cost.
- **Actively managed funds**, run by a manager trying to beat a benchmark, usually at a higher expense ratio.

Expense ratios matter more than they seem to at first glance — a fund charging 1% annually versus 0.05% can mean a meaningfully different balance after decades of compounding, since that fee is deducted every year regardless of performance. ## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Cashing out a 401(k) when changing jobs.** This triggers income tax and, if you're under 59½, typically a 10% early withdrawal penalty. Rolling the balance into a new employer's plan or an IRA avoids both.
- **Never increasing your contribution rate.** Many plans offer auto-escalation, which bumps your contribution percentage by a point or two each year — a low-friction way to increase savings as your salary grows.
- **Ignoring fund fees.** Reviewing the expense ratios on your plan's fund menu, not just picking the first option, can make a real difference over decades.
- **Leaving free employer money unclaimed.** Contributing less than the amount needed to get the full match is the single most common way people leave value on the table.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?

At minimum, contribute enough to get the full employer match — it's an immediate, guaranteed return. Beyond that, many planners suggest working toward 10–15% of income (including any match) for retirement, adjusted for your age, other savings, and goals.

### Can I have both a 401(k) and an IRA?

Yes. They're separate accounts with separate contribution limits, and you can contribute to both in the same year, though your ability to deduct traditional IRA contributions or contribute directly to a Roth IRA may be limited by income if you're also covered by a workplace plan.

### What happens to my 401(k) if I leave my job?

Vested funds are yours to keep. You can typically leave the money in the old plan (if the balance is large enough), roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Cashing out early generally triggers taxes and a penalty.

### Is a 401(k) match considered part of my compensation?

Effectively, yes — it's value your employer provides on top of salary, but only if you contribute enough to trigger it. Many people evaluate job offers partly on the strength of the match formula and vesting schedule.

### Should I choose Roth or traditional if I'm not sure about my future tax bracket?

Splitting contributions between both is a reasonable way to hedge that uncertainty, assuming your plan allows it. There's no single right answer — it depends on assumptions about your future income and tax rates that nobody can know with certainty.
