Income tells you what moved through your hands last year. Net worth tells you what you actually kept. It is the only personal finance metric that accounts for everything at once — the savings, the mortgage, the retirement accounts, the credit card you have been carrying since March — and collapses it into one figure you can track over time.

The arithmetic is trivial. What makes net worth useful or useless is the discipline behind each line: balances instead of payments, current market values instead of hopeful ones, and a definition that stays stable between updates. This guide covers how to build the statement, where distortions creep in, and how to read the result.

## The formula, and what belongs on each side

Net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe:

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

On the asset side, count things you own that have real resale or redemption value:

- Cash: checking, savings, money market, CDs
- Investments: taxable brokerage accounts, individual stocks and bonds, index funds
- Retirement accounts: 401(k), 403(b), traditional and Roth IRAs, HSAs
- Real estate: your home and any rental or investment property, at current market value
- Vehicles, at current depreciated value
- Business equity, and genuinely marketable valuables

On the liability side, list the outstanding balance of every debt:

- Mortgage and home equity loan or HELOC balances
- Student loans
- Auto loans
- Credit card balances
- Personal loans, medical debt, and taxes owed but not yet paid

That last item is the one most people forget, and for anyone self-employed or sitting on gains they intend to realize, it can be large. Tax owed but not yet remitted is a liability like any other.

What to leave off: furniture, clothing, electronics, and the rest of your household goods. Technically they have value; practically they are illiquid, hard to value, and depreciate to nearly nothing. Count an asset only if you could plausibly sell it for a knowable price.

Once you have the two lists, the [Net Worth Calculator](/apps/net-worth) does the arithmetic and keeps the categories straight — a consistent structure is what makes this quarter's number comparable to last quarter's.

## Valuing each line honestly

Four mistakes account for most distorted net worth statements.

**Using payments instead of balances.** A mortgage with $287,000 of principal remaining is a $287,000 liability, regardless of whether the monthly payment is $1,400 or $2,900. Net worth is a snapshot of what you owe today, not what you pay monthly.

**Overestimating the house.** An online estimate is a starting point, not an appraisal. Use it, subtract the mortgage balance to get true equity, and remember that selling costs take a meaningful bite out of the headline value. If you want to be conservative anywhere, be conservative here.

**Overvaluing the car.** A vehicle belongs on the statement, but only at a current, realistically depreciated value, with the auto loan sitting opposite it as a liability. A financed new car frequently shows up as a net negative for the first few years, which is accurate rather than pessimistic.

**Letting the definition drift.** If you include your car this quarter and drop it next quarter, the change in your net worth no longer means anything. Pick a definition, write it down, and keep it.

The liability side is also where the fastest progress is available. Paying down a high-rate credit card raises net worth by exactly the amount paid and removes the interest that was dragging on it — a guaranteed return no investment can promise. If you are carrying several balances, the [Debt Payoff Calculator](/apps/debt-paydown) shows which ordering clears them fastest.

## Where the number sits: Federal Reserve benchmarks

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances is the most authoritative picture of U.S. household wealth. In the 2022 survey — the most recent complete release, with the 2025 survey results to follow — median net worth for all families was **$192,900**, up 37% from $141,100 in 2019. Mean net worth was **$1,063,700**, up 23% from $868,000.

That gap is the first thing to understand about any wealth statistic. The mean runs more than five times the median because very large fortunes pull the average upward; the median — the midpoint, where half of families fall above and half below — is the one that describes a typical household.

Median net worth by age of the family's reference person, from the same survey:

| Age | Median net worth |
|---|---|
| Under 35 | $39,000 |
| 35–44 | $135,600 |
| 45–54 | $247,200 |
| 55–64 | $364,500 |
| 65–74 | $409,900 |
| 75+ | $335,600 |

The shape matters more than any single row: net worth climbs through working life, peaks in the mid-60s to mid-70s, then declines as retirees spend down what they accumulated. That decline is the plan working, not failing.

Use these as orientation, not as a grade: they say nothing about your cost of living, whether you have a pension, or how long you have been earning.

## Reading the trend instead of the level

A single net worth figure is nearly meaningless. The direction and slope across several updates is the actual signal.

Two forces move it: your savings rate — what you add from income each month, the part you control directly — and compounding on what you have already set aside, which starts small and eventually dwarfs the contributions. Running your balance and monthly addition through the [Compound Interest Calculator](/apps/compound-interest) makes that handoff visible, and usually explains why early progress feels so slow.

Expect quarters where the number falls. Markets drop, a transmission fails, a tax bill lands. None of that is a failure of the plan unless the trend over years is flat or down.

Once the trajectory is established, net worth feeds the more interesting question: how much is enough. The [Coast FIRE Calculator](/apps/coast-fire) takes your invested balance and time horizon and shows the point at which existing savings, left alone to compound, would carry you to retirement without further contributions.

## Liquid net worth, the second number worth keeping

Total net worth answers "what am I worth," not "what could I actually reach." Someone whose $400,000 net worth is almost entirely home equity and retirement accounts has very little available in an emergency.

Liquid net worth counts only what converts to cash in days without a penalty — cash, savings, and taxable brokerage holdings — minus your liabilities. It deliberately excludes home equity, which requires selling or borrowing to access, and retirement accounts, which carry tax and penalty costs before age 59½.

Tracking both separates a balance sheet that looks healthy from one that is also resilient: the gap between the two figures is a proxy for how much of your wealth is genuinely available to you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does my primary residence count toward net worth?

Yes. Your home's current market value goes on the asset side and the remaining mortgage principal goes on the liability side; the difference is your home equity. Because that equity is illiquid, most people also track liquid net worth separately so spendable and tied-up wealth do not get confused.

### Should I include my car?

Include it at a current, realistically depreciated value, with any auto loan listed as a liability. Cars are depreciating assets, so this line should shrink every year. If you cannot find a defensible value, leaving it off both sides beats guessing high.

### Is a negative net worth a problem?

Not necessarily, and it is common early in adult life. Student loans are the most frequent cause: borrowers finish school owing a substantial balance while owning little of comparable value. What matters is the direction of travel — negative and improving each quarter is a working plan; negative and widening is the signal to act.

### Do I include my pension or future Social Security?

No. A defined-benefit pension and Social Security are future income streams, not assets you own and could sell, so the standard balance sheet — including the Federal Reserve's own net worth measure — leaves them out. Account for them when planning retirement income, but keep them off the statement. Balances in 401(k)s and IRAs are different: you own those outright and they do count.

### How often should I update it?

Quarterly is enough for most people. It is frequent enough to catch a developing problem and infrequent enough to keep you from reacting to short-term market swings. The more important discipline is using the same definitions and the same valuation sources each time.
