Index funds are the closest thing personal finance has to a consensus recommendation. They're low-cost, require no stock-picking skill, and have quietly outperformed most actively managed alternatives over long stretches of time. This guide covers how they actually work, how to choose your first few, and the mistakes that trip up new investors most often.

It's written for someone who has never bought a fund before and wants a clear, no-jargon starting point — not a stock-picking strategy or a guide to timing the market.

## What an index fund actually is

An index fund is a pooled investment — either a mutual fund or an exchange-traded fund (ETF) — built to track a market index rather than to beat it. A fund tracking the S&P 500 simply holds all 500 companies in that index, in roughly the same proportions as the index itself. There's no manager trying to pick winners; the fund's return is, minus a small fee, the index's return.

That "minus a small fee" part is the whole appeal. Because there's no research team trying to outguess the market, index funds cost far less to run than actively managed funds. According to 2025 industry data from the Investment Company Institute, index mutual funds carried an average expense ratio of about 0.05% a year, and index equity ETFs averaged about 0.14%, versus roughly 0.89% for actively managed mutual funds. Some providers now offer S&P 500 index funds with expense ratios of 0.03% or lower, and a handful of zero-fee index funds exist as well. Over decades, that fee gap compounds into a large difference in ending balance — a large part of why index funds have become the default recommendation for long-term investors.

## ETF or mutual fund: which structure to use

Both structures can track the same index and hold nearly identical underlying stocks, but they behave a little differently:

- **Index mutual funds** price once per day after markets close, work well for automatic recurring investments, and are common defaults inside 401(k) plans and IRAs.
- **Index ETFs** trade throughout the day like a stock, typically have no minimum beyond the price of one share (or a fraction, on platforms supporting fractional shares), and tend to be slightly more tax-efficient in a taxable account.

Neither structure is universally "better" — the more important decision is which fund and index you choose, not whether it's wrapped as an ETF or a mutual fund.

## Building a portfolio: the three-fund approach

A popular, low-maintenance way to build a diversified portfolio is the "three-fund portfolio," popularized by followers of Vanguard founder John Bogle. It typically combines:

1. A total U.S. stock market (or S&P 500) index fund
2. A total international stock market index fund
3. A total bond market index fund

The exact mix depends on age, risk tolerance, and time horizon — a common starting point is something like 50-60% U.S. stocks, 20-30% international stocks, and the remainder in bonds, with the bond allocation typically rising as retirement gets closer. The goal isn't a specific ratio; it's broad diversification across company size, geography, and asset class using just a handful of funds instead of a large basket of individual stocks.

A single S&P 500 fund is a reasonable, very common starting point for a first-ever investment, but on its own it only covers large U.S. companies — it skips small-cap U.S. stocks and international markets entirely, which is worth knowing as your portfolio grows.

## What kind of return to actually expect

Since 1928, the S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% a year, including dividends reinvested; measured only since the index expanded to 500 companies in 1957, the average is closer to 10.5%. More recent multi-decade windows land in a similar range — the trailing 20-year average through the end of 2025 was around 11%, and the trailing 30-year average was around 10.3%.

Two things matter more than that headline number: it's an average, not a typical year — actual annual returns swing widely, and a handful of unusually strong years do most of the work — and it's a pre-inflation, pre-tax figure, so real, spendable growth will be lower, especially in a taxable account. Treat 10% as a rough long-run planning anchor, not a promise for any given year or decade.

## Lump sum vs. dollar-cost averaging

If you have a chunk of cash to invest — a bonus, an inheritance, savings sitting in cash — you'll face a choice: invest it all at once (lump sum) or spread it out over several months (dollar-cost averaging, or DCA).

The data leans toward lump sum. Because markets trend upward over most periods, investing immediately has outperformed dollar-cost averaging roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the time in historical studies, since cash you drip in over time simply misses more market days. DCA tends to look better specifically when markets are falling through the investment window, since you end up buying at progressively lower average prices.

For most people the tradeoff is emotional as much as mathematical: DCA smooths out your entry price and makes it easier to invest without agonizing over timing, even if it statistically leaves a little return on the table. If a large lump sum would keep you up at night, spreading it over a few months is a reasonable compromise, not a mistake. For ongoing paycheck contributions — how most people actually invest — you're already dollar-cost averaging by default.

## Where to hold index funds

Account choice matters as much as fund choice. Index funds inside a 401(k), traditional IRA, or Roth IRA grow without annual tax drag from dividends or capital gains distributions — a meaningful advantage over decades. A taxable account still makes sense once you've captured any employer match and used up tax-advantaged space, or if you want funds accessible before retirement age. ## Mistakes that quietly cost beginners the most

- **Trying to time entry.** Waiting for a dip, or panic-selling during one, is one of the most common ways investors underperform the very funds they own.
- **Thinking one fund equals full diversification.** An S&P 500-only fund holds no small-cap U.S. stocks and no international exposure, which is more than half the investable global market.
- **Chasing recent performance.** Switching funds because something else did better last year tends to lock in buying high and selling low.
- **Over-rebalancing.** Once or twice a year is typically enough; frequent rebalancing mostly adds cost and taxable events without improving returns.
- **Ignoring fees on "active" alternatives.** A fund charging 1% a year versus one charging 0.05% needs to outperform by that margin every year just to break even — most don't, consistently.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much money do I need to start investing in index funds?

It depends on the platform. Many ETFs can be bought for the price of a single share, and brokers that support fractional shares let you start with just a few dollars. Some index mutual funds have minimum initial investments, often in the $0-$3,000 range, though many waive minimums inside retirement accounts.

### Is a single S&P 500 index fund enough, or do I need more than one?

It's a reasonable starting point and captures most of the U.S. stock market's value, but it excludes small-cap U.S. stocks and all international markets. Many investors add a total international fund and a bond fund as their portfolio and time horizon grow, following something like the three-fund approach.

### What's the difference between an index fund and an ETF?

An index fund is a strategy — tracking a market index rather than trying to beat it — that can be built as either a mutual fund or an ETF. Mutual funds price once daily and suit automatic recurring contributions; ETFs trade all day like a stock and tend to be slightly more tax-efficient in taxable accounts.

### Should I invest a lump sum all at once or spread it out?

Historically lump-sum investing has outperformed spreading contributions out over time in roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of studied periods, mainly because markets trend upward more often than not. Spreading it out is still reasonable if it helps you actually follow through.

### How often should I check or rebalance my index fund portfolio?

Once or twice a year is enough for most investors. Index investing is meant to be low-maintenance — frequent trading or rebalancing tends to add cost and taxable events without improving long-term returns.
