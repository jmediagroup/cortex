Net worth is the closest thing personal finance has to a single scorecard. It's not your salary, your credit score, or your account balances in isolation — it's everything you own minus everything you owe, distilled into one number you can track over years. This guide covers how to calculate it correctly, what counts as "good" at different ages, and the habits that turn a one-time snapshot into a tool you actually use.

## How to calculate your net worth

The formula is simple: **assets minus liabilities**.

**Assets** — anything you own that has monetary value:
- Cash in checking and savings accounts
- Investment and brokerage account balances
- Retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, etc.)
- Home value (use a recent estimate, not the purchase price)
- Vehicle value (Kelley Blue Book or similar)
- Other significant property (a business stake, valuable collectibles)

**Liabilities** — anything you owe:
- Mortgage balance
- Auto loan balances
- Student loan balances
- Credit card balances
- Any other personal loans or outstanding debt

Add up your assets, subtract your liabilities, and the result is your net worth. It can be negative — that's common and expected earlier in life, especially for anyone carrying student loan or mortgage debt against a smaller asset base. ## What counts as a "good" net worth by age

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances is the most authoritative source on household net worth in the U.S., though it's worth noting the most recent published data was collected in 2022 (adjusted for inflation in most current summaries); the next full survey results are expected in late 2026. With that caveat, inflation-adjusted median net worth by age bracket looks roughly like this:

| Age bracket | Median net worth |
|---|---|
| Under 35 | ~$39,000 |
| 35–44 | ~$135,600 |
| 45–54 | ~$247,200 |
| 55–64 | ~$364,500 |
| 65–74 | ~$409,900 |
| 75+ | ~$335,600 |

Two things matter more than the raw numbers. First, use the **median**, not the average — the average U.S. net worth is over $1 million, but that figure is pulled sharply upward by a small number of very high-net-worth households. The median gives a far more realistic picture of a typical household. Second, treat these as reference points, not targets. Your number depends heavily on where you live, whether you own a home, student debt load, and career stage — a benchmark table can't account for any of that.

## How often to track it (and with what)

Consistency matters more than the tool. A spreadsheet updated every month is more useful than an auto-syncing app checked twice a year. Pick one approach and stick to a schedule:

- **Spreadsheet**: full control, nothing shared with a third party, but requires manual updates.
- **App with account linking**: convenient and always current, at the cost of connecting your financial accounts to a third-party service.
- **Calculator you revisit periodically**: a lightweight middle ground — you control the update cadence and don't need to link accounts.

Pick a recurring date — the first of the month, payday, the last day of the quarter — and update on that same date every time. The specific day matters far less than doing it on a fixed schedule, since the value of net worth tracking comes from the trend line, not any single snapshot.

## Common net worth tracking mistakes

A few errors show up repeatedly and quietly distort the number:

- **Using gross home value instead of equity.** Track your home's value minus your remaining mortgage balance and, ideally, estimated selling costs — not the sale price alone.
- **Forgetting small liabilities.** Credit card balances, medical bills, and small personal loans are easy to leave out and can meaningfully overstate your position.
- **Overvaluing illiquid assets.** Collectibles, art, or a private business stake are real assets, but inflated estimates make your net worth look stronger than it is. When in doubt, value conservatively.
- **Tracking sporadically.** Checking once a year makes it hard to tell whether a dip is a market downturn or an actual change in your financial habits.
- **Ignoring market fluctuations in retirement and brokerage accounts.** These balances move with the market — a single bad month isn't a trend, but a sustained multi-quarter pattern is worth paying attention to.

## How to actually grow the number

Net worth increases in exactly two ways: assets go up, or liabilities go down. In practice, most progress comes from combining both:

- **Pay down high-interest debt.** Every dollar of principal paid off is a dollar of net worth gained, immediately and with certainty. The [Debt Payoff Calculator](/apps/debt-paydown) can help you compare an avalanche vs. snowball payoff order and see how much interest you'd save either way.
- **Let investments compound.** Consistent contributions to retirement and brokerage accounts, held over time, benefit from compounding returns — small, regular contributions can grow substantially over a couple of decades. The [Compound Interest Calculator](/apps/compound-interest) makes it easy to see how contribution amount, time horizon, and return rate interact.
- **Build home equity deliberately.** Extra principal payments and long-term appreciation both add to the equity side of your balance sheet, though appreciation isn't guaranteed and varies significantly by market.
- **Avoid lifestyle inflation that erases progress.** Rising income that gets absorbed entirely by higher spending doesn't move net worth. Tracking spending separately helps you see where raises are actually going.

None of these moves need to be dramatic. Net worth is a slow-moving number by design — the goal of tracking it isn't to see monthly leaps, but to confirm the trend line is pointed the right direction over quarters and years.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I include my car in my net worth calculation?

Yes, but value it conservatively and update it periodically, since vehicles depreciate steadily. If you have an auto loan, only the equity (current value minus loan balance) contributes positively — the loan itself is a liability.

### Is a negative net worth a bad sign?

Not necessarily. It's common for people early in their careers or carrying student loan and mortgage debt to have a negative or low net worth. What matters more is the direction it's moving over time, not the number at any single point.

### How does net worth differ from income?

Income is what you earn in a given period; net worth is what you've accumulated after spending, saving, and debt payments over your entire financial life to date. It's possible to have a high income and low net worth, or the reverse.

### Should I count retirement accounts I can't access yet?

Yes — retirement accounts are real assets even though early withdrawal typically carries penalties and tax consequences. Just don't treat that money as available for near-term spending; it's part of your long-term net worth, not your liquid savings.

### How often should I recalculate my net worth?

Monthly or quarterly is a common cadence for most people — frequent enough to spot trends, infrequent enough that normal market noise doesn't feel alarming. Avoid checking so often that short-term investment swings distract from the long-term trend.
