Net worth is the single number that best summarizes your financial health — more useful than your salary, your credit score, or your account balances viewed in isolation. This guide covers how to calculate it correctly, what's typical at each age based on Federal Reserve data, the mistakes that quietly distort the number, and what actually moves it over time.

## What net worth actually measures

Net worth is simple arithmetic: everything you own (assets) minus everything you owe (liabilities).

**Assets** should include anything with real, current market value:
- Cash in checking and savings accounts
- Investment and brokerage accounts
- Retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, etc.)
- The current market value of your home and other real estate
- The current resale value of vehicles
- Ownership stake in a business

Personal items like clothing, furniture, and electronics are typically excluded, since they don't convert back into cash at anything close to what you paid.

**Liabilities** are every debt you carry, counted at its full outstanding balance — not your monthly payment:
- Mortgage balance
- Auto loans
- Student loans
- Credit card balances
- Any other personal loans or lines of credit

Subtract liabilities from assets and you have your net worth. It can be negative — that's common and expected early in adulthood, especially for anyone carrying student loans or a large mortgage relative to their home equity.

## What's typical: net worth by age

The most authoritative source for this data is the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), a triennial survey of U.S. households. The 2022 survey — the most recent one published, with the next results expected in late 2026 — put median household net worth by age of the household head at:

| Age group | Median net worth |
|---|---|
| Under 35 | $39,000 |
| 35–44 | $135,600 |
| 45–54 | $247,200 |
| 55–64 | $364,500 |
| 65–74 | $409,900 |

The overall median across all U.S. households was about $192,700. Note this is the *median* — the household squarely in the middle of the distribution — not the average. Average net worth figures are pulled sharply upward by a small number of very wealthy households, which makes median the far more useful benchmark for comparing yourself to a "typical" household.

These numbers are a snapshot, not a target. They tell you where households actually land, not where you should be. A more prescriptive benchmark comes from retirement-savings guidelines like Fidelity's, which suggest aiming to have saved roughly 1x your annual income by age 30, 3x by 40, 6x by 50, 8x by 60, and 10x by 67 — assuming a consistent savings rate and a mostly stock-based portfolio during your working years. That's a narrower measure (retirement savings, not full net worth), but it's a useful gut check alongside the broader SCF figures. ## Common mistakes that distort the number

A few errors show up constantly when people calculate net worth for the first time:

1. **Valuing assets at purchase price instead of current value.** A car bought for $32,000 might realistically be worth $19,500 two years later. Use current market value, not what you paid.
2. **Recording debt at the monthly payment instead of the balance.** A $30,000 auto loan with a $450 monthly payment is a $30,000 liability, not a $450 one.
3. **Forgetting accounts.** Old 401(k)s from a previous employer, a savings account you rarely check, or a small brokerage account opened years ago are easy to leave out — and they add up.
4. **Skipping the liability side entirely.** Listing only assets inflates the picture and defeats the purpose of the exercise.
5. **Reacting to short-term market swings.** A volatile month in the stock market can move a portfolio-heavy net worth up or down substantially without reflecting any real change in your financial habits or trajectory.

## How to actually grow your net worth

Net worth moves in exactly two directions: growing assets or shrinking liabilities (ideally both). A few levers do most of the work:

- **Pay down high-interest debt.** Every dollar of principal paid off is a dollar of net worth gained, and it's guaranteed — unlike investment returns. If you're carrying credit card or loan balances, the [Debt Payoff Calculator](/apps/debt-paydown) can show how much faster an avalanche or snowball strategy gets you to zero.
- **Let compounding work on the asset side.** Consistent contributions to retirement and investment accounts benefit enormously from time in the market. The [Compound Interest Calculator](/apps/compound-interest) makes it concrete: the difference between starting at 25 versus 35 is often hundreds of thousands of dollars by retirement, even at identical contribution rates.
- **Build home equity deliberately.** For most households, home equity is a major share of net worth. Extra principal payments, or simply holding a property while the mortgage balance shrinks and market value grows, both count.
- **Automate contributions.** Households that treat saving and investing as a fixed, automatic expense — rather than whatever's left over — tend to build net worth more consistently than those who save opportunistically.

## How often to track it

Once a year is the minimum — enough to see the trend without obsessing over noise. Many people find quarterly tracking (say, aligned to the end of March, June, September, and December) hits a good balance: frequent enough to catch problems early, infrequent enough that normal market fluctuations don't feel alarming. Monthly tracking can help build the habit when you're just starting out, but for most people it isn't necessary — and can lead to overreacting to short-term swings that have nothing to do with your actual financial decisions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal to have a negative net worth?

Yes, especially in your 20s or early 30s if you're carrying student loans or recently took on a mortgage. Federal Reserve data shows median net worth is relatively low for households under 35 for exactly this reason — it typically turns positive and grows steadily as debts are paid down and savings accumulate.

### Should I include my home in my net worth?

Yes, at its current market value, with the outstanding mortgage balance subtracted as a liability. What you should track separately is home equity (value minus mortgage balance) if you want to understand how much of your net worth is illiquid real estate versus more accessible assets.

### Does net worth include retirement accounts I can't touch yet?

Yes. Retirement accounts like a 401(k) or IRA are real assets even though early withdrawal typically triggers taxes and penalties. Some people choose to track "liquid net worth" (excluding retirement accounts and home equity) as a separate figure to see what's actually accessible in an emergency.

### Why is average net worth so much higher than median net worth?

Average net worth is skewed upward by a relatively small number of very high-net-worth households. Median net worth — the household exactly in the middle of the distribution — gives a far more realistic picture of what a "typical" household actually has, which is why the Federal Reserve and most researchers emphasize median figures.

### What's a good net worth goal for my age?

There's no single universal target, since it depends on income, location, and life circumstances. The Federal Reserve's median figures by age group show what's typical, while frameworks like Fidelity's income-multiple guidelines (1x income by 30, 3x by 40, and so on) offer a more aspirational benchmark tied to retirement readiness.
